How do you know when you are ready to get off the tools?

Check how the work you currently do will be covered, including the time needed for supervision and training. Review the costs and workload before adding commitments.

Identify a capable person for the defined task. Check their capacity, give them the information they need and agree when to involve you.

Plan the handover in this order

A phased transition beats a clean break. Each step should prove itself before the next one starts.

  1. Name one responsibility and the person taking it on.
  2. Agree the scope, decision limits, records and escalation route.
  3. Demonstrate the task and work through it together.
  4. Trial the handover with suitable oversight.
  5. Review the result before expanding responsibility.

Start with the work that still depends on you

If you run an established UK building company, getting off the tools usually means changing your role gradually. You may still visit sites and make important decisions, while your team takes more responsibility for everyday delivery. The aim is to create time for estimating, client relationships, planning and developing people without leaving a gap on site.

For one working week, record what pulls you back into hands-on work. Separate planned work from interruptions: a missing delivery, an unclear drawing, an unanswered client question or a decision nobody feels authorised to make. Look for repeated causes. More time in the office will not help if every routine decision still waits for your phone call.

Check whether the business can support the change

Map the work you intend to hand over and the capacity needed to cover it. Include supervision, training and your own management time. Review job costs and the timing of receipts and payments before committing to extra capacity. A busy order book alone does not show whether the change is affordable. Your job-pricing process should reflect how you will actually deliver the work.

There is no universal turnover figure at which every builder should leave the tools. An owner running one complex refurbishment faces a different handover from an owner overseeing several extension teams. Base the next step on the people, workload and oversight your own jobs need.

Choose one responsibility and one capable person

Start with a defined area, such as coordinating agreed deliveries or maintaining the daily progress record. Choose someone whose ability you have seen, and check that they have time to take it on. A good craftsperson may need support before coordinating other people. Be clear about what they can decide, what needs approval and who covers their absence.

Show the task, do it together, then let them lead while you observe. Ask them to explain the decisions and the information they would need if something changed. Our construction delegation guide develops this approach. If nobody has the necessary capacity or skills, address that gap before removing your own involvement.

A handover checklist for a domestic extension

For example, a capable site lead could coordinate deliveries already agreed for a rear extension. Keep the scope specific while they learn the routine.

  • Owner and scope: name who confirms delivery dates and keeps the team informed.
  • Decision limits: explain what they can arrange within the agreed plan and what needs approval.
  • Escalation: identify who to contact immediately about safety concerns, missing materials, changed specifications or delays affecting other work.
  • Records: keep delivery updates and unresolved decisions in the shared project record.
  • Check-ins: agree a review cadence suited to the work and the person's experience.
  • Proof: check that records are accurate, problems are raised in time and the agreed task is covered before expanding the role.

Keep site oversight and client communication clear

A handover needs more than a group chat. Keep the current programme, drawings, responsibilities, agreed changes and open decisions somewhere the relevant people can access. Name who maintains each record. Agree when progress is reviewed and which issues require immediate escalation rather than waiting for the next meeting.

Introduce the day-to-day contact to the client and explain which matters still come to you. Confirm who can agree changes to scope, cost and programme. For example, the site lead might record a request to move an internal wall, while the appropriate people review its implications before any change is approved or carried out.

Stepping back from manual work does not remove the need for competent supervision. HSE's contractor guidance for Great Britain explains the need to plan, manage and monitor construction work and provide suitable supervision, information and instruction. Keep the required oversight in place as roles change.

Trial protected management time, then review what happened

Choose a manageable period away from hands-on work on a job where suitable cover is in place. Tell the team how to reach you for decisions that need your involvement. Use that time for a named business priority, such as reviewing estimates or planning the next project, rather than allowing it to disappear into miscellaneous admin.

Afterwards, review the work with the person who took responsibility. What progressed as planned? Which decisions stalled? Were instructions missing, authority unclear or the workload unrealistic? Fix the cause before increasing the time or scope. If quality, communication or delivery slips, reduce the trial and strengthen the support.

A simple weekly review

  • Owner time: planned site work, unplanned call-backs and protected management time actually used.
  • Delivery: agreed milestones, unresolved decisions, rework and outstanding quality checks.
  • People: whether the lead had enough time, information, skills and cover.
  • Commercial progress: estimates followed up, changes recorded and job costs reviewed.
  • Clients: updates delivered and questions resolved by the agreed person.

Use these observations to decide the next handover. Fewer phone calls are only helpful if the work and communication remain sound. You are looking for a team that knows when to act and when to ask.

Your first four weeks

  1. Week 1: record where your time goes and choose one recurring responsibility to hand over.
  2. Week 2: agree the scope, decision limits, records and support with the person taking it on.
  3. Week 3: run a limited trial with suitable supervision and a clear review point.
  4. Week 4: review delivery, client communication and your time. Strengthen the handover before expanding it.

This is a starting exercise, not a promise that you can leave the tools in a month. The pace depends on what the trial demonstrates.

Questions UK builders ask about getting off the tools

Do I need a site manager before I step back?

You need capable people covering the responsibilities you are handing over. A site manager may be appropriate, but a job title alone does not prove readiness. Define the role, assess competence and capacity, and provide the support and supervision the work requires.

How long does getting off the tools take?

There is no fixed timetable. Start with one responsibility and increase the handover when delivery and communication hold up. A team with established processes may be ready for a different pace from one that still relies on the owner for routine decisions.

Can I keep doing some hands-on work?

Yes. You can choose to retain selected work while building reliable cover for the rest. Make that a planned choice, with enough time left for the responsibilities only you can fulfil as the owner.

What if the team keeps calling me?

Record what each call was about. Repeated questions can point to missing information, unclear authority, a training need or insufficient capacity. Address the pattern together. Keep escalation open for issues that genuinely need your decision.
